What's new

5 Oct 2026

  • 48-hour outlook: see the wind and rain for every hour ahead and tap an hour to plan the route for that time.
  • Mission types: Delivery (land or drop at the destination, lighter on the way back, one flight plan each way) and Photo / inspection (stay airborne at the destination, same weight both ways, one round-trip flight plan), each with a time at the destination.
  • Two plans for every route: Fastest, and Least battery, which can hold its altitude over rolling terrain instead of following every hill, to avoid costly climbs.
  • Battery estimate: round-trip battery use for the recommended heights (and every row of the table), from each drone's rated flight time, payload, climbs, wind and temperature — with a warning when less than a 20% reserve would be left.
  • Imperial units (ft, mph, mi) for routes in the US, or pick your units next to the result.
  • New satellite map with place names, plus a street-map option.
  • Country-specific rules: the route now follows the drone rules of the country it starts in (Israel and the US so far), including height limits, airport distances and Washington DC's no-drone zone.
  • Terrain-aware routes: heights are now above the ground, so over hills like the Carmel the drone climbs and descends with the terrain and never goes above 120m over the ground. New side view of the route under the result.
  • Flight plans (WPML) now carry a separate height for every waypoint, following the terrain.
  • Progress bar with an estimated time left, which learns from your previous calculations.
  • More reliable restricted-area and building lookups: backup map servers, automatic retries, and a "Try again" button.
  • More accurate wind model: crosswind correction, strong headwinds flagged as unflyable, fixes to the forecast hour and to wind direction between altitudes.
Why altitude matters
  • The right height makes deliveries faster, keeps food warmer, and saves battery or stretches your range.
  • Fly often — a delivery route, say — and small gains per trip add up fast.
  • Wind isn't uniform with height, so a route grounded by a high-wind warning near the ground can still be flyable higher up.
  • We won't recommend flying below 30m — besides clearance margins, it also cuts noise disturbance noticeably. We'll keep you at least 20m above the tallest building OpenStreetMap knows about near your route, shown in faint orange on the map — climbing over buildings is simpler and safer than weaving between them at low level.
  • We apply the drone rules of the country your route starts in — currently Israel (CAAI, 50m height limit) and the United States (FAA, 400ft / 122m). Anywhere else we fall back to Israel's rules, which are on the strict side. If you hold a permit to fly higher, tick the box in the drone settings.
  • Heights are above the ground, not above takeoff: over hills the drone climbs and descends with the terrain, stays at least the chosen height above it, and never goes above the local height limit over it. The side view under the result shows the planned altitude against the ground, from Copernicus GLO-90 elevation data (~90m resolution).
  • Schools, kindergartens, hospitals, playgrounds, nursing homes, universities/colleges and power infrastructure are risky to fly over — we mark the ones OpenStreetMap knows about in red on the map and route around them by at least 30m (or their real size, if bigger), since altitude alone doesn't make overflying them safe.
  • Airports, military sites, prisons and similar sites are a different kind of risk — flying near them can be illegal or need authorization no matter how you route around them. We use each country's own distances (in Israel, for example, 2km from airports; in the US, controlled airspace around airports needs LAANC authorization, and Washington DC has its own no-drone zone) and flag them with a separate, more prominent warning. We only check OpenStreetMap's map data, not official no-fly-zone maps or current NOTAMs/TFRs — always verify there before flying.
  • Altitude choice matters most on long routes, with higher-drag drones, and when the wind runs roughly along your heading.
  • Drag isn't the enemy — flown with the wind, it works for you.
