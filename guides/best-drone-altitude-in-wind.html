What altitude should I fly my drone in wind?

Updated 6 Oct 2026 · Flytimizer guides

Short answer: Usually not the highest allowed altitude. Wind normally gets stronger with height, so fly lower into a headwind. With the wind behind you it depends on the drone: most multirotors on a mission (DJI waypoints, for example) hold a set ground speed, so a tailwind doesn't make them faster and staying low both ways is usually best; a fixed-wing drone, or one flown flat out, does go faster, so on a long leg higher with a tailwind can pay off. Climbing also takes time and battery, which favours staying low on short trips.

Wind is stronger higher up

Near the ground, friction with terrain, trees and buildings slows the wind. Higher up it blows more freely. A common rule of thumb is the wind-profile power law: wind at height h ≈ wind at a reference height × (h / reference)α. Over open, flat ground in the daytime α is about 0.14, which makes the wind at 120 m about 20% stronger than at 30 m. Over towns or forests, and at night or early morning when the air is stable, α can reach 0.3–0.4: the wind at 120 m can be 50–75% stronger than at 30 m, and it often turns direction with height too.

Weather models forecast the wind at several heights, so you don't have to guess. Open-Meteo, for example, gives hourly wind at 10, 80, 120 and 180 m.

A headwind costs more than a tailwind gives back

Your drone flies at a fixed speed through the air. The wind adds to or subtracts from that, so a headwind slows you down and a tailwind speeds you up by the same amount. But you spend longer in the headwind, so on a round trip the losses outweigh the gains.

Example: a drone cruising at 12 m/s flies 2 km out and 2 km back.

Into the wind the drone is flat out at 12 m/s through the air, so it makes 7 m/s over the ground either way. With the wind behind, a drone that holds its airspeed makes 17 m/s; one that holds a set ground speed (like a DJI waypoint mission) stays at 12 m/s and just uses less power. Climb and descent time not included here.

That asymmetry is why the altitude matters so much on the headwind leg: every metre per second of headwind you avoid saves more time than the same amount of tailwind would add.

Different legs, different altitudes

Put the two effects together. Into the wind you want the slowest wind, which usually means lower. With the wind behind you, a stronger wind higher up only helps a drone that goes faster with it, and only if the leg is long enough to repay the climb. Here's an 8 km delivery leg each way (take off, fly, land at each end) with a 12 m/s drone that climbs at 5 m/s and descends at 3 m/s. The wind blows from the destination: 4 m/s at 30 m and 8 m/s at 120 m.

Either way, the big win is flying low into the headwind: about 40% off "just fly high". Climbing high for the tailwind leg only pays for a drone that actually goes faster with the wind. A drone holding a set ground speed gains nothing from the stronger tailwind and still pays for the climb.

On a short route the climb matters even more. With the same wind over 2 km each way, staying at 30 m both ways is fastest for both kinds of drone. There's no single best altitude. It depends on the wind at each height, the leg length, your drone's speeds, and how it flies in wind.

Crosswind and gusts set limits

  • Crosswind: to hold its track the drone has to angle into the wind, which slows it: ground speed = √(airspeed² − crosswind²). A 12 m/s drone in an 8 m/s crosswind makes only about 8.9 m/s, 25% slower. If the crosswind reaches the drone's speed, it can't hold the track at all.
  • Gusts: they're often forecast only near the ground (10 m). The gust-to-average ratio there is a reasonable estimate higher up, so if the gusts at some height reach your drone's rated wind resistance, rule that height out, even if it would be faster.

Heights are above the ground

Legal limits are measured above the ground below the drone: 120 m in the EU (EASA open category) and 400 ft in the US (FAA). Over hills, a constant altitude can put you too low over a ridge and over the limit above a valley. Plan heights above ground level along the whole route, not just at takeoff.

FAQ

Is it better to fly a drone high or low in wind?

Usually low into a headwind, because wind is normally weaker closer to the ground. With a tailwind it depends on the drone: most multirotor missions hold a set ground speed, so a stronger tailwind higher up doesn't make them faster and staying low is usually best; a fixed-wing drone, or one flown flat out, does go faster, so higher can pay off on a long leg. Check the forecast at several heights for your route.

Why is my drone's return flight faster or slower than the outbound flight?

Because the wind is a headwind one way and a tailwind the other. The drone flies at the same airspeed, but its ground speed is airspeed minus the headwind or plus the tailwind. A round trip in wind always takes longer than in calm air, because more time is spent in the headwind.

How much stronger is the wind at 120 m than at 30 m?

Typically about 20% stronger over open ground in the daytime, and 50–75% stronger over towns, forests, or at night and early morning when the air is stable. It varies hour by hour, so use a forecast that gives wind at several heights.

What wind speed is too much for my drone?

Check your drone's rated maximum wind resistance (for example, "Level 5, 10.7 m/s"). Compare it with the gusts, not just the average wind, at the height you plan to fly, which is usually stronger than the ground-level figure in your weather app. Also, a crosswind or headwind close to your drone's cruise speed leaves it unable to make progress.
